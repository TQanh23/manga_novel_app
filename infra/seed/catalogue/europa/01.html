The beacon pulsed every eleven seconds, a slow blue heartbeat in the black water. Ines Okafor had counted those pulses for four hundred and twelve days, and she still found them comforting.

Station Halcyon hung beneath forty kilometres of ice, anchored to a ridge of volcanic rock. Above her, the frozen crust of Europa groaned and shifted with Jupiter's tides. Below her, the ocean went down another hundred kilometres into a dark no probe had ever reached.

Her job was simple. Keep the beacon lit. Log the cargo submarines as they passed, hauling ice cores and microbial samples up to the drill shaft. Report anything unusual. In fourteen months, nothing had been unusual.

She ate dinner at the observation window, as she always did, a tray of rehydrated lentils balanced on her knees. Outside, a drift of pale particles sank through the floodlights like snow falling upward in reverse.

At 21:40 station time, the sonar chimed. A contact, eight kilometres out and closing, moving at a speed no cargo sub could manage. Ines set down her fork and checked the registry. The contact carried no transponder at all.

She did what the manual said. She hailed it on every channel, in English, Mandarin and the old maritime code. The contact slowed, circled the beacon once, and stopped exactly eleven seconds of light away from her window.

Then the speakers crackled, and something answered. It was not a voice. It was a pattern of clicks and long, falling tones, and it repeated the rhythm of her beacon back to her, perfectly, pulse for pulse.
