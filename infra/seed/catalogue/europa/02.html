Ines did not sleep. She recorded the signal, slowed it down, ran it through every analysis tool the station had. The structure was unmistakable: it had grammar, repetition, variation. Whatever sent it was talking.

She drafted a report to the surface and then deleted it. A report meant a quarantine team, a military submarine, the beacon switched off for good. It meant the end of whatever this was before it had properly begun.

At dawn, which on Halcyon meant nothing but a change in the lighting schedule, the contact was still there. She could make out its shape now in the floodlights: long, ribbed, faintly translucent, like a lantern made of glass and bone.

Ines switched the beacon to manual. Instead of the steady eleven-second pulse, she sent three short flashes, then a long one. A greeting, or as close to one as she could invent.

For a long moment nothing happened. Then lights bloomed along the creature's flanks: three short, one long. And then a new pattern, slower and more complicated, that she did not understand at all.

Her console beeped. A cargo submarine, the Persephone, was requesting guidance through the ridge, ninety minutes out. Its crew would see the visitor. Everyone would know.

Ines looked at the patient glowing shape beyond the glass, and at the blinking request on her screen, and realised that for the first time in four hundred and thirteen days, she had no idea what the right thing to do was.
