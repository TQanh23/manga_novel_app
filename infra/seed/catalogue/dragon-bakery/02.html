By autumn, the bakery had regulars. Bertram came every morning for a sourdough loaf and pretended he had never run away screaming. The blacksmith's wife ordered honey cakes by the dozen. Pip had learned to stop flinching when Ember sneezed.

Then the poster went up on the village notice board: THE ANNUAL HARVEST FESTIVAL BAKE-OFF. GRAND PRIZE: THE GOLDEN WHISK. Underneath, someone had scrawled, NO DRAGONS.

"That is discrimination," Ember said, reading it for the fourth time. "Also, it is spelled wrong. They have written dargons." Pip pointed out that it was probably the work of Madame Fontaine, who had won the Golden Whisk nineteen years in a row.

The village council met to discuss the matter. It was the longest meeting in recorded history, mostly because Ember had brought pastries and nobody wanted to leave. In the end, they voted seven to two that dragons could enter, provided they did not breathe on anyone else's entry.

On festival day, Ember baked a seven-tier apple tart shaped like a castle, complete with spun-sugar towers. It was, everyone agreed, the most magnificent thing they had ever seen. Then she sneezed, and the castle caught fire, which Ember felt was a little too on the nose.

The judges tasted what was left. It was caramelised, smoky and astonishing. Madame Fontaine tasted it too, went very quiet, and then, to everyone's surprise, asked for the recipe.

Ember did not win the Golden Whisk. It went to the blacksmith's wife, for her honey cakes, which had been baked using Ember's oven temperatures. But that evening, Madame Fontaine came by the bakery with a bottle of cider, and the two of them argued about butter until well past midnight, which Ember decided was even better than winning.
